Motivation (The Why)

In modern Chinese society, the rapid pace of urbanization and digital transformation has created a growing emotional gap between generations. Younger family members are deeply immersed in digital culture, while grandparents often feel disconnected from the fast-evolving technological world. Traditional family gatherings, such as the Chinese New Year's Eve dinner, remain one of the few moments where all generations come together. However, even during these precious occasions, meaningful intergenerational interaction is often lacking — younger members may be absorbed in their phones while elders sit quietly [1].

Research shows that the mere presence of mobile devices degrades in-person social interaction quality [1], while intergenerational storytelling has been proven to strengthen family identity and emotional bonding [2]. Game-based approaches have also shown promise in fostering communication between generations in family settings [3]. Yet existing communication tools like WeChat lack dedicated features for cross-generational shared activities, elder-friendly interfaces, or collaborative experiences that leverage each generation's strengths.

Bridge of Time aims to transform these shared moments into playful, engaging experiences that celebrate cultural heritage while fostering genuine emotional connections across generations. By designing a mobile web application with inclusive, accessible interactions, we hope to create a "bridge" that connects the wisdom of the past with the energy of the present.

Interview Insights (5 Grandparents, aged 60+)

Q: How often do you see your grandchildren?
"Not very often. Their parents are busy with work, and we don't live close by. We usually only get together for dinner on weekends."
Q: Do you find it easy to chat on your phone?
"Not really. The text is too small, my eyesight is bad. There are too many functions, and I can't remember how to use them all."
Q: What's the biggest difficulty talking to grandchildren?
"No common topics! I don't understand the games, influencers, or internet slang they talk about, and they're not interested in my stories."
Q: What kind of tool would help you communicate better?
"I just want something simple! Big text, big buttons, no learning curve. Ideally one-tap video calls and voice messages."
Q: What do current chat apps lack?
"Intergenerational interaction features! They're all made for young people, with no content designed for grandparents and kids to use together."

The Gap — Literature Review (4 Academic Papers)

We reviewed academic literature on intergenerational communication, digital play, and inclusive design. For each paper, we identify what they did well and what gaps remain.

Did Well
  • Empirically proved phones degrade face-to-face interaction quality
  • Clear experimental design with control groups
  • Highlighted the "mere presence" effect of devices
Missed

  • Does not address mobile platform constraints
  • Limited sample diversity (single cultural context)

Key Requirements

From our user research, these are the 3 things our app absolutely needs to get right

1

Shared Collaborative Activities

The system must provide activities that require both generations to participate together — neither can complete the task alone. This creates positive interdependence [5] and gives both generations a reason to engage. Research shows that game-based interactions effectively foster intergenerational communication [3]. Our survey shows 63.2% want to share photos/stories together, and 36.8% want simple games to play together.

Survey: 63.2% want shared activities Interview: "I wish we could do something together"
2

Elder-Friendly Accessible Interface

The interface must use large touch targets, high-contrast visuals, simple navigation, and voice input as a primary interaction mode for elderly users [6]. 42.1% of respondents say ease-of-use is the most valued feature, and interview participants consistently described current apps as "too small" and "too complicated."

Survey: 42.1% prioritise ease of use Interview: "Big text, big buttons, open and use right away"
3

Reversed Knowledge Hierarchy

The system must include features where grandparents are the experts — sharing traditions, recipes, and cultural knowledge — reversing the typical tech-teaching dynamic [7][8]. This restores elders' sense of value and gives grandchildren genuine reasons to listen, not out of politeness but curiosity.

Literature: Reverse mentoring boosts elder self-efficacy [8] Interview: "Nobody wants to listen to my old stories"

Ideation & Design Alternatives

We considered multiple directions before settling on our final design

Crazy 8s Sketching

Our team conducted rapid ideation sessions using the Crazy 8s method, generating 8 quick UI concepts in 8 minutes. Key ideas that emerged: voice-first storytelling, collaborative cooking timers, cultural trivia battles, and shared photo timelines.

Alternative A

Video-Call Add-on

A WeChat mini-program that overlays interactive games on video calls between grandparents and grandchildren.

Pros: Leverages existing platform, low friction to adopt
Cons: Limited by WeChat API restrictions, no standalone identity, poor offline support
Rejected: too dependent on third-party platform constraints
Alternative B

AR Family Scrapbook

A tangible-first approach: physical photo album that triggers AR animations when scanned with a phone camera.

Pros: Physical artifact grandparents can hold, novel XR experience
Cons: Requires physical printing, high tech barrier for elders to initiate AR, limited scalability
Rejected: tech barrier contradicts elder-accessibility requirement
Chosen

Playful Activity Hub (Bridge of Time)

A standalone mobile web app with role-based UI that provides collaborative activities (stories, cooking, quiz, album) designed for same-room use during family gatherings.

Pros: No install needed (PWA), role-based complexity, interdependent tasks, works offline, elder-friendly UI
Cons: Requires both parties to have a phone, no real-time sync between devices (single-device prototype)
Chosen: best balance of accessibility, engagement, and technical feasibility

Prototypes & Iteration

What we changed after getting feedback, and why

Iteration 1

Elder Accessibility — Large Font Mode

When we had peers test the app as grandparent users, the most common complaint was about readability — text was too small and buttons were hard to tap accurately.

Before

  • Fixed 12-14px font sizes across all screens
  • Standard touch targets (36px)
  • No accessibility options available

After

  • Large Font Mode scales text by 30-50%
  • Touch targets enlarged to 52px+
  • Settings accessible from role selection screen (⚙️ gear icon on Grandparent card)
  • Context-based: uses React Context to propagate across all screens
This made the app actually usable for our target grandparent users. It directly addresses Requirement #2 (Elder-Friendly Interface) and the survey finding that 42.1% of respondents said ease of use matters most to them.
Iteration 2

Playful Interaction — Quiz Celebration

The quiz just... ended. After the last question you got a plain "Quiz Complete" message and that was it. It felt anticlimactic — especially for something that's supposed to be a fun shared moment between grandparent and grandchild.

Before

  • Quiz ended with a simple "Quiz Complete" text
  • No emotional reward or celebration
  • Score shown in plain text

After

  • Confetti animation plays on quiz completion
  • Animated score reveal with encouraging messages
  • Role-specific praise (acknowledging grandparent as "tradition expert")
  • Replay and share options for continued engagement
The celebration moment turned the quiz from a "test" into a shared achievement. It reinforces the idea that play can be a social lubricant between generations — which is the whole point of our app.

Evaluation

How we tested our prototype and what we learned from it

Method

We ran an informal peer evaluation with 3 classmates who hadn't seen the app before. Each person played through the prototype as both a "grandchild" and a "grandparent" user, completing a set of core tasks while we observed. Afterwards, they gave us verbal feedback on what felt natural, what was confusing, and what they'd want to see improved.

Task Completion Results

Task Success Rate Avg. Time Notes
Navigate from splash to Home screen 3/3 (100%) ~8s All found it intuitive
Enable Large Font Mode 2/3 (67%) ~15s 1 evaluator initially missed the gear icon
Complete a story listening session 3/3 (100%) ~20s Voice player metaphor well understood
Complete the Traditions Quiz 3/3 (100%) ~45s Confetti animation praised as "delightful"
Add a photo to Memory Album 3/3 (100%) ~12s Timeline layout intuitive

What worked well vs. what needs fixing

We also did a quick heuristic walkthrough based on Nielsen's usability principles:

Strengths

  • Clear navigation — back buttons on every screen, you always know where you are
  • Familiar metaphors: photo album timeline, step-by-step recipe, quiz cards
  • Warm, consistent visual style that doesn't feel "techy" or intimidating
  • Users always have control — can go back, change settings, skip ahead

Things to improve

  • No confirmation dialog if you accidentally leave a story recording halfway through
  • No onboarding — first-time users have to figure things out themselves
  • The gear icon for Large Font Mode isn't obvious enough (1 out of 3 testers missed it initially)

What testers said

"The large font mode is exactly what my grandma would need. The gear icon could be more prominent though."
"I love the confetti at the end of the quiz — it makes you want to play again!"
"The Cook Together screen really feels like something you'd do side-by-side. The role division is clever."

Reflection

Looking back at what we built, what it means, and how AI helped

Social & Ethical Impact

Bridge of Time tries to address something we noticed in our own families — grandparents and grandchildren sitting at the same dinner table during Spring Festival but barely talking, because they don't share the same digital world. Our app gives them a reason to interact: cooking together, sharing stories, quizzing each other on traditions. The grandparent isn't just "helped" — they're the expert.

We paid attention to inclusivity from the start. The Large Font Mode and simplified UI for the grandparent role aren't afterthoughts; they came directly from our persona research on Grandma Wang. We didn't want elderly users to feel like they were using a "dumbed-down" version — their interface is just as rich, just physically easier to use.

On privacy: family stories and photos are personal. A real production version would need end-to-end encryption and local-first storage. For this prototype we sidestepped the issue entirely — it's fully stateless, no data is stored anywhere. That was a deliberate choice, not laziness.

One limitation we're aware of: the app centres on Spring Festival and dumpling-making, which reflects Han Chinese culture. Not every family in China celebrates the same way. If we developed this further, we'd need to support more diverse cultural backgrounds and avoid making assumptions about what "family tradition" looks like.

AI & Vibe Coding Reflection

We used Claude (Anthropic) as our main coding assistant for building the React prototype. To be clear: all design decisions, user research findings, personas, and interaction logic came from us. The AI was a tool for translating our specifications into working code faster — it didn't decide what to build or why.

In practice, we used it for four things: scaffolding the React screen components from our Figma wireframes, building the accessibility system (FontContext + Large Font Mode), generating the quiz celebration animation, and configuring deployment to GitHub Pages. Each time, we wrote a detailed prompt describing exactly what we wanted, reviewed what came back, and adjusted or rewrote parts that didn't fit.

Specific Reflection on AI-Assisted Development

Example 1: Large Font Mode (Accessibility Feature)

What we asked: We prompted Claude to create a React Context for global font scaling, and to place a settings toggle on the Grandparent card's top-right corner as a gear icon, with all font sizes increasing by 30–50% and touch targets enlarging to 52px+.

What happened: The generated FontContext.tsx worked well and all seven screens were migrated correctly. But the first version put the toggle on the HomeScreen instead of the RoleSelectScreen — which didn't match our intended flow where accessibility should be set up before entering the main experience.

What we did about it: We gave a more specific instruction pointing to "the Grandparent role card in RoleSelectScreen." The second attempt was correct. We then tested with 3 peers acting as grandparent users to check that the gear icon was easy to find, text scaled without overflow, and touch targets were large enough.

Example 2: Quiz Celebration Animation

What we asked: We wanted a confetti celebration when the user finishes all quiz questions — CSS keyframes only, no libraries. Colourful particles falling, an animated score counter, and encouraging text to make it feel rewarding.

What happened: The animation looked great and matched what we had in mind. But it spawned 50 particles with heavy transform sequences, which caused visible lag on older phones during testing.

What we did about it: We cut the particle count to 30, added will-change: transform for GPU acceleration, and simplified the keyframes. After that it ran smoothly on both iPhone 12 and Pixel 6 emulators. Testers said it "makes you want to play again" — so the optimisation didn't hurt the emotional effect.

Ethics of using AI-generated code

We were conscious of three things throughout this process:

  • Accessibility can't be an afterthought — we double-checked that AI-generated components actually met WCAG standards for our elderly users (font sizes, contrast, touch targets), rather than just trusting the output
  • We reviewed all quiz content and story prompts for cultural bias — the AI occasionally generated content that felt stereotypical about elderly people, and we rewrote those parts
  • Everything is documented — the /ai-logs folder has the key prompts we used, so anyone can see exactly where AI was involved
